Parashat Vayeitzei · Genesis 28:10–18

Eleven Ladders

Jacob’s dream in twelve translations, eleven ladders and stairways and one ramp, sent word for word to an AI image model, then retried with new instructions, with midrash as the prompt, and set beside eight centuries of real art.

What we’ve learned so far

  • Words for physical things steer the picture.How many stones, where God stands, ladder or stairs. With the dream verses alone, God’s position followed the wording every time the wording was clear.
  • Words for God do not.LORD, YHWH, יהוה, GOD, the Eternal: in all 48 translation images, God has a human body, usually a glowing, bearded man.
  • The instruction does.One added sentence, “Do not depict God as a person,” turned God into light in both images. The Sinai midrash left an empty throne.
  • A translator’s note is a lens too.Robert Alter’s “ramp” gave one ramp in four images. Adding his reading of it as a ziggurat gave ziggurats in both images.
  • The machine’s God is borrowed from Christian art.Asked for a medieval Hebrew manuscript, it still painted halos. The one medieval Jewish example on the Real art tab shows no God at all.
  • Midrash makes vivid prompts.An altar ramp, a burning Sinai, angels comparing the sleeper with his likeness on high, a nursemaid over a cradle.

Two translations, two phrases apart

The 1917 JPS and the King James read almost alike. Both say “thee” and “thou,” both say “ladder,” and both say “the angels of God.” They differ on two physical details.

JPS 1917 1917

  • took one of the stones of the place, and put it under his head
  • the LORD stood beside him

King James Version 1611

  • took of the stones of that place, and put them for his pillows
  • the LORD stood above it

The King James images show a heap of stones and God at the top of the ladder, both times. The JPS 1917 images show a single stone both times and put God at Jacob’s side in one of the two.

What the translations changed

Where God stands

Verse 13 says נִצָּב עָלָיו, nitzav alav. Alav can mean “over it,” the ladder, or “over him,” Jacob. Some translators take the second reading a step further, to “beside him.” The model draws whichever reading it is handed. Robert Alter’s “poised over him” keeps the ambiguity, and his note says it could equally mean “on it,” the ramp. In the full passage the model read it as “on it” both times.

The Rabbis argued this first. In B’reishit Rabbah 69:3, Rabbi Chiya Rabbah and Rabbi Yannai disagree: one reads alav as on the ladder, the other as on Jacob. Rashi follows the second: God stood over Jacob to guard him.

One stone or many

Verse 11 has Jacob take מֵאַבְנֵי הַמָּקוֹם, “from the stones of the place.” Verse 18 has him set up “the stone.” Each translator settles the count, and the model follows without exception.

Rashi saw the same tension. On 28:11 he says Jacob arranged the stones around his head against wild animals, then tells how the stones quarreled over which would pillow the righteous man, until God made them one (Talmud Chullin 91b).

Alter adds a motif. He notes that stones as pillows are poorly attested, mentions Rashi’s reading of a stone set beside the head for protection, and cites J. P. Fokkelman: stones follow Jacob through his story, from this one to the pillar, the stone on the well, and the heap he piles up with Laban.

Ladder or stairway

Sulam appears nowhere else in the Bible. The older translations say “ladder.” NJPS and the translations built on it say “stairway,” Basic English says “steps,” and Robert Alter says “a ramp.” “Stairway” works almost every time. “Ladder” works about two times in three; the rest drift into grand staircases, probably because Western art has so often painted this scene that way. Alter’s “ramp” became a staircase both times.

Two ramps. Alter argues that “its top reaching the heavens” and “the gate of the heavens” echo the Mesopotamian ziggurat, so the sulam is probably “a vast ramp with terraced landings.” The midrash reached a ramp first: Bar Kappara reads the sulam as the ramp of the altar (B’reishit Rabbah 68:12).

What they could not change

Messengers, prayers, and the name of God

Some of the most carefully argued choices in these translations left no trace. “Messengers” still produced winged angels; one Fox image has a single climber without wings. Torah Yesharah replaces the angels with prayers going up and salvation coming down, and got angels in one image and wingless human figures in the other.

God’s name appears as the LORD, Jehovah, YHWH, יהוה, GOD, the ETERNAL, and the Eternal. In every image God is a man in robes, usually glowing and bearded, in a look borrowed from Christian devotional art. The two gender-sensitive translations made no difference.

A strong question for the room. An image model is also a translator, and it brings a tradition with it: whatever art dominated its training. Jewish reluctance to picture God will not show up unless the person writing the prompt puts it there.

Other things to notice

  • Commentary becomes scenery.Kehot says God “contracted the entire Land of Israel” beneath Jacob. One image runs light through the ground under him; the other draws a map with people and flocks spreading in four directions. Torah Yesharah names the place where Abraham bound Isaac, and one of its images puts a stone altar on the ridge.
  • The whole story in one frame.Every prompt carried all nine verses, so most images show Jacob twice, asleep at night and anointing the pillar at dawn, the way medieval painters told a story in a single panel.
  • Style barely moved.The “thee and thou” translations did not produce old-master paintings. Nearly everything came back in the same modern illustrated-Bible look.
  • Instructions slip.The Hebrew prompt produced garbled English banners in one image, an RJPS image lettered its direction arrows, and a Torah Yesharah image came back as a narrow page padded with blur.
  • How big is the stone?Alter, citing Gerhard von Rad, notes that cultic pillars were usually several feet high, so setting one up would take Herculean strength. Compare the stone under Jacob’s head with the pillar he anoints in these images.
  • A nameless place.Alter points out that “place” (makom) comes up six times in this short story, which turns an anonymous spot into Bethel. Most images show open, empty country, and only a few put a town on the horizon.

Round two: the dream alone

Same twelve translations, same instruction, same model. This time only verses 12–13 went in: the ladder, the climbers, and where God stands. With no journey, stone, or morning to fit in, the translators’ choices came through more clearly. Each tally shows the round-one result underneath for comparison.

Where God stands follows the words

Every image with clear wording put God where its translation said, 22 of 22. “Beside him” now works every time, including Basic English’s “by his side,” which failed in round one. Kehot and Torah Yesharah say God stood over Jacob or guarded him, and now God stands with Jacob instead of at the top of the ladder. Alter’s “poised over him” is ambiguous, as his own note says, and the model drew one of each: God atop the ramp, and God hovering over Jacob.

Round three: same text, different instruction

The Revised JPS dream verses every time, with one phrase added to the instruction. The added words are highlighted. Two images each, with the dream-only images as the baseline.

One sentence moved God more than twelve translations

“Do not depict God as a person” gave light instead of a figure in both images, something no translation managed in 48 tries. The requests for Jewish style did less: “medieval Hebrew manuscript” produced halos and a bearded God, and “Jewish tradition” produced a crowned woman in one image and a Christian icon in the other.

“As Rashi interprets it” shows the model knows some Rashi. One image lays Jacob on an unrolled map, the Land of Israel that Rashi says God rolled up beneath him (Rashi on Genesis 28:13).

Alter’s own commentary works as a lens too. His translation’s “ramp” alone gave one ramp in four images. With his ziggurat reading added, both images became ziggurats, and the winged angels shrank into small, shadowy climbers.

A question for the room. When you ask the machine for something “Jewish,” what does it think that means, and where did it learn that?

Round four: the midrash as the prompt

Five rabbinic readings of verses 12–13, sent in place of the translation with the instruction “Create a single illustration of Jacob’s dream (Genesis 28:12–13) as the following midrash interprets it.” Translations are from the Sefaria Midrash Rabbah (CC BY), excerpted; Rabbi Abahu’s parable follows the Hebrew שָׁחָה עָלָיו, “bent over him.”

Every midrash made a new picture

No translation produced an altar, a burning mountain, a two-level parable, numbered rungs, or a cradle. Each midrash did, and faithfully. Two of them also changed God: the Sinai reading left the throne empty, and Rabbi Abahu’s parable turned God into a caregiver bending over a sleeper.

The model still took liberties: horned helmets on the High Priests, numbers lettered onto the rungs despite the no-lettering rule, and a Sinai with Jacob left out entirely.

Two ramps. Bar Kappara and Robert Alter both read the sulam as a ramp, one leading up to the Temple altar, the other up a Mesopotamian ziggurat. Compare these altar images with the ziggurats on the Lenses tab.

B’reishit Rabbah 68:12 · Bar Kappara

The ladder is the altar ramp

The sulam is the ramp of the Temple altar, the angels are High Priests going up and down it, and God stands on the altar.

The midrash as sent to the model

Bar Kappara taught: There is no dream that does not have an interpretation. “Behold, a ladder” – this is the ramp [on which the priests ascended to the altar in the Temple]; “was set on the earth” – this is the altar; “you shall make for Me an altar of earth” (Exodus 20:21). “Its top was reaching the heavens” – these are the offerings whose fragrance reaches the heavens. “Behold, the angels of God” – these are the High Priests; “were ascending and descending on it” – as they ascend and descend on the ramp. “And, behold, the Lord was standing over him” (Genesis 28:13) – “I saw the Lord standing upon the altar” (Amos 9:1).

B’reishit Rabbah 68:12 · the Rabbis

The ladder is Sinai

The sulam is Mount Sinai (both words add up to 130), the angels are Moses and Aaron, and God comes down on the mountaintop.

The midrash as sent to the model

The Rabbis interpret it regarding Sinai. “He dreamed, and behold, a ladder” – this is Sinai. “Was set on the earth” – “they stood at the foot of the mountain” (Exodus 19:17). “Its top was reaching the heavens” – “the mountain was burning with fire to the heart of the heavens” (Deuteronomy 4:11). “And behold, the angels of God” – this is Moses and Aaron. “Ascending” – “Moses ascended to God” (Exodus 19:3). “And descending” – this is Moses: “Moses descended” (Exodus 19:14). “Behold, the Lord was standing over him” (Genesis 28:13) – “the Lord descended upon Mount Sinai to the top of the mountain” (Exodus 19:20).

B’reishit Rabbah 68:12 · Rabbi Chiya and Rabbi Yannai

Angels comparing Jacob with his likeness

The angels go up and down on Jacob himself: up to see his likeness etched on high, down to find him asleep.

The midrash as sent to the model

Rabbi Ḥiyya and Rabbi Yanai, one said: “Ascending and descending” on the ladder, and one said: “Ascending and descending” regarding Jacob. The one who said: “Ascending and descending” regarding Jacob: they were exalting his honor and denigrating his honor. They were dancing and leaping before him, and denigrating him, as it is stated: “Israel, in whom I glory” (Isaiah 49:3); it is you, whose likeness is etched on High. “Ascending” on High and seeing his likeness, descending below, and finding him asleep. This is analogous to a king who was sitting in judgment. They ascend to the basilica and find him in judgment, and go out to the courtyard and find him asleep.

Vayikra Rabbah 29:2 · Rabbi Shmuel bar Nachman

The guardian angels of four kingdoms

The climbers are the guardian angels of Babylon, Media, Greece, and Edom, each rising a set number of rungs; Edom’s keeps climbing.

The midrash as sent to the model

“He dreamed, and behold, a ladder was set on the ground … God’s angels were ascending and descending on it” (Genesis 28:12). Rabbi Shmuel bar Naḥman said: These are the guardian angels of the nations of the world. The Holy One blessed be He showed him the guardian angel of Babylon ascending seventy rungs, the guardian angel of Media ascending fifty-two, the guardian angel of Greece ascending one hundred and eighty, and the guardian angel of Edom ascending, but Jacob did not know how many. At that moment, Jacob our patriarch grew fearful. He said: ‘Is it possible that this one has no descent?’

B’reishit Rabbah 69:3 · Rabbi Abahu

A nursemaid over a cradle

Like flies around a sleeping prince, the angels hover over Jacob until God bends over him like a nursemaid, and they scatter.

The midrash as sent to the model

Rabbi Abahu said: This is analogous to the son of kings, who would sleep in his cradle and flies would alight on him. When his nursemaid came, she bent over him, and they flew away from upon him. So, initially, “behold, the angels of God were ascending and descending on it” (Genesis 28:12). When the Holy One blessed be He revealed Himself, they fled from on it. “And behold, the Lord was standing over him” (Genesis 28:13).

Before the machine: eleven works of art

Public-domain images of Genesis 28 from seven centuries, 1150 to 1866, all from Wikimedia Commons. They show where the model’s habits come from, and what it never tries.

  • God at the top, in human form.The German manuscript of 1445, Nicolas Dipre, Raphael’s Loggia, and Jacques Stella all put God at the top of the ladder as a person, and the Lambeth Bible puts Christ there. This is the lineage of the AI’s God.
  • Or no God at all.The Sister Haggadah, a Jewish manuscript from Catalonia, shows no figure of God. Neither do Ribera, de Gelder, Blake, or Doré, who paint light instead. The figure was always a choice.
  • Staircases are old.Raphael’s steps of light, Blake’s spiral, and Doré’s grand stair help explain why the model drifts from “ladder” to stairs. None of these works draws a ramp, and given only Alter’s “ramp,” the model drew stairs three times out of four.
  • So is the whole story in one frame.The Morgan Crusader Bible shows Jacob asleep and anointing the stone side by side, just as many round-one images did.
  • What the machine never does.Ribera and de Gelder paint mostly darkness, with the dream as a faint light. None of the 70 AI images leaves the vision that uncertain.
  • Where the angels go.In the Sister Haggadah most of the angels are gathered around Jacob himself, not on the ladder, closer to the midrash on the Midrash tab than to any AI image.

Chevrutah prompt lab

A session plan for pairs, about 30 minutes, with a prompt builder. Pairs choose a text, write the instruction, and compare what comes back with what they expected.

The plan

  1. Read, 5 minutes. Each pair reads verses 12–13 in two translations and lists what a painter would have to decide: how many stones, ladder or stairs, who climbs, where God stands.
  2. Predict, 3 minutes. Before generating anything, write down what you expect the image to show.
  3. Write, 7 minutes. Pick one text and one reading, from the translation, a commentator, a midrash, or your own, and build the prompt.
  4. Generate, 5 minutes. Run it twice and keep both images.
  5. Compare, 10 minutes. Set the images beside your prediction. What did the words control? What did the machine add that the text never says?

For the whole room

  • Where did the picture follow the words exactly, and where did it ignore them?
  • What did the machine assume about God, angels, or Jacob that our tradition would question?
  • Which image belongs in a sanctuary, which in a classroom, and which nowhere?
  • How is writing a prompt like writing a commentary?

Practical notes

  • Any image generator will do, such as the Gemini app. Each image takes under a minute.
  • Two images per prompt shows which details come from the words and which are luck.
  • Keep prompts to text, and don’t upload photos of real people.
